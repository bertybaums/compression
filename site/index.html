Doing Philosophy in a Thousand Words

We tried to build a machine that reasons about philosophy using only the thousand most common English words. It failed in a way we misdiagnosed for months. This is the story of the misdiagnosis — and of how we caught it.

1 · The question

In 1913, Henry Sheffer proved something startling: every operation in classical logic — and, or, not, all of it — can be built from a single connective. One stroke. You lose nothing you could say. You only pay in length: what took one line now takes twenty.

That trade — lose vocabulary, keep meaning, pay in length — is the question this project asks about ordinary language. Take the roughly one thousand most common English words. Randall Munroe called this Up Goer Five, and used it to describe a Saturn V rocket without the word "rocket."

Can you do philosophy in it? Not describe philosophy — do it. Draw a distinction. Raise a counterexample. Follow an argument where it goes.

Primer: what "the thousand words" actually means here

Our word list has entries, of which are alphabetic words (the rest are punctuation and digits). It is bigger than exactly 1,000 because it includes inflections — "think", "thinks", "thinking" are separate entries. The spirit is Munroe's: only words a general audience uses without thinking about them.

2 · The naming wall

Here is the first thing we did not expect, and we found it by accident — while trying to get a large model to write us specific philosophical cases in Up Goer Five. About 92% of what it wrote was rejected. Not for being vague. For naming things.

Try to write one specific case below — a particular situation, with particulars. Not "when helping others costs you," but one case, with details that make it that case.

Try it: write one specific case

Give it a real go before reading on. The point is what you hit.

If you reached for a name — Ali, Maria, John — it was rejected. Every time. There is not one proper name in the entire word list.

Some other things go too, but be careful about why. farmer, wallet, charity and noon are all rejected — yet doctor, car, school and teacher are perfectly fine. That's not a principle; that's just word frequency. A thousand words happens to include "doctor" and happens not to include "farmer."

But the names are not a frequency accident. There are zero. A thousand-word vocabulary cannot name at all. It can only describe.

But look at what our generator produced once it stopped fighting this. These are real cases from the corpus, and they are perfectly particular:

"The man in the white shirt will give the small silver key to the woman in the black dress at the station."

"If a car must hit one person or five people, should the driver turn the wheel?"

The trolley problem, nameless. Particularity survives — but it has to arrive as definite description rather than as a name. Which is to say: the word list enforces Russell's theory of descriptions — not as a philosophical position you argue for, but as the only way left to talk.

Primer: Russell's theory of descriptions, in one paragraph

Bertrand Russell argued in 1905 that phrases like "the present King of France" don't work by pointing at a thing. They work by describing: there is exactly one thing that is a King and is of France, and it is bald. Philosophers have argued about whether names are secretly descriptions ever since. Here that argument doesn't get to happen — a thousand words contains almost no names, so description is not a theory. It's the only road.

This is the Sheffer trade showing up in an unexpected place. We expected to pay in length. We also paid in reference.

3 · The instrument

To test the question we built a small language model — about 200 million parameters, trained from scratch, that has never read a word outside the list. Two stages:

  1. Pretraining. Read ~2 million passages of Up Goer Five reasoning. Learn what the language looks like.
  2. Instruction tuning (SFT). See ~400,000 pairs of prompt → response. Learn to answer when asked.

Then we test it on prompts it has never seen, and a blinded judge scores every answer 0–4 on three things: does it engage the question actually asked, is it coherent, does it have substance.

Primer: why "blinded" and why it matters more than it sounds

The judge never learns which model wrote what — the files have opaque names and no labels. That's standard. Less standard, and something we measured: we re-scored identical text six weeks apart and the scores moved differently by dimension. "Did it answer the question?" replicated exactly. "Was there real philosophical work?" moved by more than a full point on a four-point scale. Objective-ish questions are stable; taste questions drift. We report the stable one as the headline.

4 · It works. Sort of.

Ask it something in the shape it was trained on, and it is genuinely good — close to the 120-billion-parameter teacher that taught it. Ask it something in a new shape and it produces fluent, well-formed, grammatical Up Goer Five about something else entirely.

5 · The hunt

If the model won't read the prompt, something must be stopping it. We spent months eliminating suspects. Each row below is weeks of work and real compute.

Five suspects. Five acquittals. That should have been the clue.

Primer: what "eliminating a suspect" means

You change one thing and hold everything else fixed. If the failure doesn't move, that thing wasn't the cause. It's the logic of a controlled experiment, and it's sound — with one catch that this whole page is about. Eliminating suspects only works if the real culprit is among the suspects. Five acquittals can mean you've narrowed it down. Or it can mean you're searching the wrong building.

7 · The twist

Our 400,000 training examples contained different prompts. Each one appeared about times, paired with a different answer every time.

So the prompt never identified an answer. It identified a bucket. And reading it any more closely than "which of the 380 is this?" earned the model exactly nothing. Not a little. Nothing.

The model didn't fail to learn prompt-attention. It learned that prompt-attention was worthless — because in that corpus, it was.

Here is the evidence that settles it. Our test set happens to contain two kinds of question: five types the model saw during instruction tuning, and three types it saw only in pretraining. Same model, same test, same day:

Not a gradient. A cliff. Inside its buckets the model is out of 4 — nearly teacher quality. Outside them, . Nothing in between.

It isn't a broken reasoner. It's a 380-way classifier with an essay generator bolted to each slot — which is the optimal policy for the data we gave it. It learned exactly what we paid it to learn. We just hadn't noticed what we were paying for.

And now re-read the hunt. Capacity, vocabulary, two kinds of post-training, response form — every one of them was an attempt to make a lookup table read. Not one of them changed the thing that made lookup optimal. Five acquittals, because the culprit was never in the lineup.

Being honest about this result

The three types the model can't do also come from different source files than the five it can. So "wasn't in instruction tuning" is tangled with "came from somewhere else," and we can't fully separate them from this data alone. Two things make us think the bucket story is right anyway: the three failing types come from two different pipelines yet land on the same floor, and 0.12 out of 4 isn't "harder" — it's absent. But the clean test is cheap and we're running it: drop one of the five good types out of training and see whether it falls off the same cliff. If it doesn't, we're wrong.

The decisive experiment is running as you read this: a corpus with hundreds of thousands of different prompts instead of 380. If prompt-reading was never worth anything before, is it worth something now? We don't know yet. That's what makes it worth running.

8 · What this teaches

The result is interesting. The mistake is more useful.

Check what the data rewarded

Before concluding a system can't do something, ask whether anything it was trained on ever made doing it worthwhile. We asked "why won't it read prompts?" for months. The answer was: because we never once paid it to.

A name is a hypothesis

We called it the "prompt-attention deficit." Deficit means a weak thing to strengthen — so we spent months trying to strengthen it. The model didn't have a weak capability. It had a rational strategy. Every lever we picked was picked by the name.

Don't control a mediator

We nearly forced the machine to write fixed-length answers to remove a confound. But in a thousand-word vocabulary, length is part of what we're studying — saying something specific costs words. Clamping it would have hidden the effect we were looking for.

Measure it; don't inherit it

Our own design document said the training answers ran "about 317 words." They run 285. 317 is the median of a different experiment, copied across at some point and never checked. Every number on this page is computed from the data files at build time, because the alternative is how 317 happened.

None of these are lessons about language models. They're lessons about looking. The model was never confused. We were.
